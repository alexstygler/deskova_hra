Current direction · updated 12 November 2026

A Search Tonight

Confirmed choice: the designer selected A Search Tonight, set in Kilmainham Gaol, as the leading concept. This is a working title and an early direction, not a finished or tested game.

Setting: the prison while it was functioning, with its architecture, people, routines, and history shaping play. The museum/exhibition approach was explicitly rejected.

Historical possibilities: Parnell’s imprisonment and the Kilmainham agreement of 1882, or imprisonment following the Easter Rising of 1916. The scenario and period have not been selected.

Core idea: an inspection is approaching, but players have incomplete information about its target, timing, or conditions. They pursue individual objectives while deciding how much limited effort to spend becoming better informed and how much to commit to making progress.

Central decision to explore: “Spend effort on a clue and make a smaller attempt, or commit more effort with incomplete information?” This is a proposed decision structure, not a settled action sequence.

Next focus: research the closest competitors using the prompt below. The next design step is to define prisoners’ individual objectives and the limited opportunities they compete for, then describe one complete provisional round.

No playable prototype, age rating, pacing result, sales forecast, or originality claim has been validated.

Design goals we are carrying forward

  • Approximately 2–9 players, each playing for themselves. Target about 40 minutes at 2–4 players and no more than about 50 minutes at nine. Measure decision and resolution time separately.
  • Simple rules with depth: roughly Splendor as a rules-complexity reference; quick to explain, with several viable strategies and interesting choices from the beginning.
  • Other players matter: their pursuit of opportunities changes your options, giving you reasons to adapt. Avoid an isolated puzzle against the guards.
  • Mixed public and private information; simultaneous decisions are a preferred direction for reducing waiting. Neither guarantees fast resolution by itself.
  • Limited luck: a little randomness in available opportunities and outcomes is welcome, but decisions should dominate. Setbacks should remain recoverable.
  • Comfortable social play: room for casual conversation, without required trading or negotiation. Avoid constant time pressure, excessive memorization, and players sitting out.
  • Competition through self-benefit: an action may disadvantage others as a consequence of helping yourself. Avoid actions mainly intended to damage someone, repeated victimization, and player-controlled traitors.
  • Satisfying decisions: finding non-obvious solutions, anticipating opponents, and making effective combinations all appeal. No priority order has been selected.
  • Audience hypothesis: adult and teenage appeal, with possible accessibility to families with children around 10+. This is untested; no final minimum age has been chosen.
  • Physical quality: satisfying components, with Splendor’s chips as a tactile reference. No component list or budget is fixed.
  • User-led originality: the designer’s ideas should shape the game. Reference games illustrate enjoyable experiences; borrowing their mechanics is optional.

References: Splendor, Azul, City of the Great Machine, chess, Rubik’s Cube solving, and Texas Hold’em. The designer particularly likes logical decisions and responding to opponents, and dislikes repetitive compulsory buildup. Colt Express was mentioned by an external report, not named by the designer as a favorite.

How we work: explore one focused topic at a time; record conclusions; distinguish confirmed preferences from proposals; finish each topic with the next focus. An HTML notebook, later including a web prototype and design experiments, is the agreed working format.

Search Tonight — proposals and open questions

Fictional prisoners or supporting characters could operate within a historically grounded scenario. The guards and inspections would be controlled by the game system. A player-controlled traitor is outside the current direction.

Possible individual objectives

  • Get a message to an outside contact.
  • Obtain access to a useful location or contact.
  • Protect or move a document before a search.
  • Combine information, access, and timing to complete a private plan.

These are examples, not adopted objective cards. Escape is not currently the central objective. Scoring and victory conditions remain undecided.

Questions that determine the game

  • Which historical period and player roles are we using?
  • What does each player want, and why do they need the same limited opportunities?
  • What clues exist, who sees them, and how does obtaining information compete with acting?
  • How do inspections resolve, and how can a player recover after an unsuccessful attempt?
  • What are the map, movement, capacity, action sequence, ties, round count, and resource-recovery rules?
  • How do we scale opportunities and resolution to nine players?

Earlier mechanics available for testing

During the opportunity-boom exploration, the designer preferred choosing an opportunity and secretly committing a limited resource, with a higher commitment winning and all commitments spent in the explained example. A known risk of no reward and a mixture of automatic and chosen extra recovery also appealed.

Status in Search Tonight: those are inherited ideas to evaluate, not automatically adopted prison rules. Exact effort spending, recovery, and the role of auctions are unresolved. Splitting bids across several locations was discussed by the external research report, not selected as our rule.

What to measure in a prototype

  • Run the same core structure at four and nine players; separate time spent deciding from time spent resolving.
  • Count full rounds with no progress or reward, particularly consecutive ones.
  • Observe whether clues support reasoning rather than constant memory work or unexplained guessing.
  • Check whether opponents influence choices without enabling repeated targeting.
  • Check newcomers’ independent decisions, recovery after mistakes, and enjoyment; audience and pacing targets remain hypotheses.

Kilmainham and historical inspiration

Historical framing: the Kilmainham Treaty was an informal political understanding in 1882, rather than a formal treaty signed in a prison ceremony. Kilmainham’s role in the 1916 setting concerns the aftermath of the Easter Rising. Keep these periods separate and verify specific movements, contacts, routines, and events before turning them into rules.

Fictional supporting characters and invented gameplay situations should be identified as such. A simplified real layout is a possible inspiration, not a committed full-building simulation.

Eight Kilmainham approaches explored — only Search Tonight selected as the lead
  1. An Agreement Without a Table, 1882: combine limited opportunities and influence into proposals meeting changing political conditions; represent negotiations through rules without mandatory player bargaining.
  2. Four Channels to the Outside, 1882: combine access to visitors, correspondence, allies, and the press to get information or requests through.
  3. Tomorrow’s Front Page, 1882: acquire reports and assemble a newspaper under space and deadline constraints. The prison is the story’s center, but most player activity is outside.
  4. The Land War Through the Walls, 1882: connect a prison hub to communities and political institutions through short projects. Scope and complexity would need tight limits.
  5. The Courtyard Window, post-1916: pursue private objectives through a shared map and prison timetable.
  6. A Search Tonight: gather clues or act under uncertainty before inspections. Selected by the designer as the clear winner; the historical scenario remains open.
  7. Before the Transfer: arrange messages, belongings, contacts, and objectives before a shared deadline.
  8. At the Final Bell: a clearly fictional alternate-history escape prepared for a final window, keeping everyone involved until the end.

The assistant preferred Search Tonight over Prague for this brief because of the information-versus-progress decision and potential shared resolution. Prague retained an advantage in immediate visual clarity and moving components. Those are design judgments, not playtest results.

Conduct a thorough, source-based competition analysis for a physical board-game concept with the working title “A Search Tonight,” set in Kilmainham Gaol.

I need an honest assessment of its closest competitors, potential originality, and commercial positioning. Search actively for games that challenge its originality. Do not assume an unusual historical setting makes the game mechanically original.

RESEARCH SCOPE

Research internationally, with particular attention to European and Czech availability. State the research date. Include older influential games, recent releases, announced games, crowdfunding projects, and relevant small-publisher or print-and-play games.

The concept below is an early design direction, not a completed or tested game.

THE CONCEPT

Players are fictional prisoners or supporting characters operating within the historical setting of Kilmainham Gaol.

The historical scenario is not yet selected. Two possibilities are:
• Parnell’s imprisonment and the political developments surrounding the Kilmainham agreement of 1882.
• Imprisonment following the Easter Rising of 1916.

Verify the historical context. Keep these periods separate. The Kilmainham Treaty should not be presented as a formal treaty signed at a prison ceremony. Distinguish documented history from invented gameplay situations.

The prison is a functioning setting during the historical events—not a museum. Its layout, restricted access, routines, and connections to the outside world should affect play.

An inspection or search is approaching, but players have incomplete information about its target, timing, or conditions. They pursue individual objectives while deciding how much effort to spend learning more and how much to commit to making progress.

Possible objectives include getting a message outside, obtaining access to a location, protecting a document, or establishing a useful contact. These are examples, not settled rules. Escape is not currently the central objective.

Players compete for useful opportunities. A rival’s pursuit of their own objective can change what is available to others. Guards and inspections are controlled by the game system, rather than by a player acting as a traitor or prison governor.

THE INTENDED PLAYER EXPERIENCE

• Logical decisions and understandable consequences.
• Deduction and calculated risk under incomplete information.
• Anticipating other players’ choices.
• Discovering effective combinations of actions, access, and information.
• Adapting when opportunities change.
• Meaningful competition without actions mainly intended to damage another player.
• Some room for casual conversation rather than constant time pressure or intense memorization.

An illustrative decision is:
“Should I spend effort obtaining a clue, leaving less for my objective, or make a stronger attempt with incomplete information?”

EXISTING DESIGN GOALS

• Approximately 2–9 players, each playing for themselves.

• At nine players, target no more than approximately 50 minutes.
• Low downtime, with simultaneous decisions a preferred direction.
• Approachable rules with strategic depth, using Splendor as a rough complexity reference.

• Several viable approaches rather than one dominant strategy.
• Limited luck and recoverable setbacks.
• No required trading, negotiation, hidden-traitor roles, or early player elimination.
• A physical board game; a web prototype would only be a development tool.

Do not treat these as completed mechanics:
• Exact objectives, scoring, and victory conditions.
• How clues work and how much players know.
• Inspection timing and consequences.
• Movement, map size, and location capacity.
• Whether secret effort commitments or auctions belong in the final game.
• Whether effort spent on an unsuccessful action is lost.
• Resource recovery, ties, round count, and player-count scaling.

Design brief — agreed preferences

Reference games

Splendor and Azul are favorites; City of the Great Machine is also enjoyable. The appeal is logical decision-making and the way one player’s actions change what others need to do. These are references for enjoyable experiences, not a commitment to copy their rules or player roles.

Other enjoyable games and activities include chess, solving a Rubik’s Cube, and Texas Hold’em poker. What specifically appeals about each is still to be explored; their inclusion does not establish a preference for bluffing, betting, hidden information, or any particular puzzle structure.

Requirements

  • An original game shaped by the designer: favor their own ideas and creative direction. Borrowing existing mechanics is optional; combining the named reference games is not the design brief.
  • Individual competition: every player plays for themselves.
  • Mixed information: combine public information with some private information or choices. What stays hidden and when it is revealed remain undecided.
  • Limited randomness in both forms: include a little randomness in the opportunities available before decisions and a little uncertainty in action outcomes, allowing calculated risks. Exact frequency and stakes remain open; luck should not dominate.
  • Secret commitment to contested opportunities: players privately commit a limited resource. The designer prefers the explained version in which a higher commitment wins the reward and both winning and losing commitments are spent. A contested choice can therefore yield no reward. Exact amounts and ties remain open; there is no decision to eliminate players.
  • Hybrid effort recovery: combine some automatic recovery with a player-chosen way to recover extra. Exact timing, amounts, caps, and the cost of extra recovery remain open.
  • Temporary opportunities as the main activity: focus on spotting and pursuing short-lived openings, then adapting to the next ones. Managing a lasting business is not the central activity. Effort management and other forms of longer-term planning remain possible.
  • Satisfying decisions: include opportunities to find non-obvious solutions, anticipate other players, and combine things in unexpected, effective ways. All three appeal to the designer; their relative emphasis is undecided, and every move need not provide all three.
  • Competition through self-benefit: an action that improves your own position may disadvantage others as a consequence. Avoid actions whose main purpose is intentionally damaging an opponent. This refines the earlier blanket restriction on harm; specific effects remain undecided. Avoid making someone feel singled out as a victim.
  • Simple rules, strategic depth: aim for roughly Splendor’s level of rules complexity, with a quick explanation that friends can understand easily.
  • Several viable approaches: repeated play should reveal interesting possibilities rather than one dominant winning strategy.
  • Responsive strategy: the best choice should depend on what opponents do. Rock–paper–scissors illustrates this relationship; it is not a request to use that mechanic.
  • Replayability and accessibility: experienced players should stay interested, while newer players retain a reasonable chance of winning.

What the game should feel like

  • Logical and deliberate: decisions and their consequences should be understandable.
  • Connected: other players’ actions should meaningfully influence your choices.
  • Satisfying to handle: value physical component quality, especially the feel of Splendor’s chips. Specific components and production budget are undecided.
  • Comfortably social: leave some mental space for friends to catch up during play; constant intense concentration should not be necessary.
  • Forgiving: a mistake should not automatically remove a player’s realistic chance of winning. How much recovery is possible remains to be tested.
  • Engaging from the opening: offer interesting choices early, without a repetitive preparation phase in which everyone feels obliged to do the same thing.

Avoid

  • Luck dominating the outcome. A little randomness in both opportunities and action outcomes is welcome.
  • Trading, bargaining, or negotiation as required gameplay. Casual conversation is welcome.
  • Continuous high-pressure concentration, such as the shape-fitting experience described in Ubongo.
  • A single dominant strategy or an early mistake deciding the whole game.
  • A repetitive opening built around everyone accumulating discounts before the interesting decisions begin.
  • Actions mainly designed to damage another player, especially repeated targeting that leaves one person upset or creates arguments. Disadvantaging others as a consequence of improving your own position is acceptable.

These are design targets, not validated properties of a game we have built.

Interaction — agreed direction

Core principle: what other players do should matter to your decisions. You can make a plan, but you need to reconsider your tactics as the situation changes.

  • Splendor example: a shared market contains a card you have been preparing to buy for several rounds. Another player takes it before your turn, changing your options.
  • Azul example: other players take tiles you wanted, changing what is available and what makes sense for you to choose.
  • The appeal: respond to opponents and changing opportunities rather than follow the same plan regardless of their actions.
  • Mechanism still open: a shared market or tile draft could produce this experience, but neither is required or chosen.

Design question for later testing: how can a disrupted plan lead to an interesting alternative while preserving our goal that mistakes and setbacks remain recoverable? At nine players, we will also need to test how much can change between meaningful decisions.

The reference examples describe the desired experience; they are not complete rules summaries.

Boundary clarified: the designer accepts harm that arises as a consequence of acting for your own benefit, but dislikes intentionally damaging others as the purpose of an action. The concern is repeated victimization, sadness, and arguments. Design around incentives for self-benefit, and test whether players nevertheless find repeated targeting rewarding. Exactly which effects are acceptable remains open; earned progress is not subject to the blanket protection previously inferred.

Progression — preference clarified

The designer does not particularly enjoy Splendor’s permanent-discount buildup. In their experience, the opening feels boring because everyone builds up in a similar way. They also perceive this as the clear winning approach in the base game without the expansion they refer to as “Silk Road.” This records their experience and assessment, not an independently established claim about Splendor’s strategy balance.

Design direction: make the opening interesting and avoid a repetitive buildup that feels compulsory. Splendor-style discount accumulation is not a feature we need to reproduce.

Still open: other forms of progression, upgrades, and combinations. This preference does not rule them all out.

Working direction — secret commitment

  1. Reveal a small set of shared opportunities.
  2. Everyone privately chooses an opportunity and commits some of a limited resource at the same time.
  3. Reveal together. For a contested opportunity, the higher commitment earns the reward; committed resources are spent even when the player gains no reward. Ties remain unresolved.

Illustrative example accepted as the preferred approach: you have five effort tokens for several rounds. You commit two to an opportunity; another player commits one to the same opportunity. You earn the reward and retain three tokens; the other player spends one without earning the reward. Five tokens and the example bids are illustrations, not balanced values or a settled resource name.

The designer selected this commitment approach over everyone failing when an opportunity is crowded. Uncertainty from hidden choices is distinct from randomness. Ties, minimum commitments, rewards, and the number and capacity of opportunities still need rules and tests, especially at nine players.

Recovery direction: combine a small automatic refill with a player-chosen way to recover extra effort. One possible implementation is choosing recovery instead of competing that round; the action cost and all numerical values are still proposals to test. Recovery should fit the shared round rhythm rather than create a long period of waiting.

Playtest question: how often does someone gain nothing, and can they recover? Accepting a missed opportunity should still fit the earlier goals of recoverable setbacks and low frustration.

Theme discovery

The designer’s interests: mathematics, history, sports, AI, video making, traveling, and entrepreneurship. “A city at a turning point” is the most exciting direction so far, with family accessibility a concern to resolve. It is the current favorite, not a finalized setting.

The designer’s theme seed — an opportunity boom

A major change happens and puts many opportunities on the table. Any of them could offer a path to becoming wealthy, but the more people pursue an opportunity, the harder it becomes. This is the designer’s own direction to explore; the cause of the change, historical era, and exact player roles are undecided.

Desired experience: spot promising opportunities, anticipate where other players will go, and adapt as competition changes their attractiveness. Competition arises from pursuing your own success, consistent with the boundary against actions mainly intended to damage others.

Activity selected: spot and pursue temporary opportunities. The designer prefers this to lasting ventures because it suits the working mechanics and the intended quick gameplay. How long each opportunity remains available is still open. Longer-term planning can still come from managing effort and anticipating later opportunities.

Working mechanism: secretly commit limited effort to a chosen opportunity, reveal together, and let higher commitment win contested rewards. Exact capacity, rewards, ties, and how congestion scales to nine players still need rules and tests. Becoming wealthy is the thematic ambition; final scoring and the win condition are not yet fixed.

Event structure selected: one main change starts the boom, and additional smaller developments create new opportunities throughout the game. Their frequency, relationship to player actions, and specific content remain open. A railway or any other particular event has not been selected.

Advance information decided: developments arrive without warning; the designer prefers this to advance hints. This does not yet specify whether events occur before or after choices within a round, nor how destructive or random their effects are. Those details must fit the existing preferences for limited luck and recoverable setbacks.

Next question: which initial change sparks ideas: a new transport connection bringing people and goods, an invention changing daily life, or a valuable discovery drawing people to the city? These are assistant-proposed theme seeds; the designer can modify them or propose another.

Design suggestion: initially try developments that change available opportunities within the same core rules, to preserve the goal of simple explanations. Whether this works needs testing.

The following are assistant-proposed seeds inspired by those interests. They are possibilities to reshape, not agreed mechanics or validated game concepts.

  • Film festival: independent filmmakers turn scenes, locations, and limited production time into a distinctive film. The satisfying challenge could be making pieces work together as a coherent story.
  • A city at a turning point: entrepreneurs in a historical city pursue opportunities as travel routes, inventions, and local needs change. The challenge could be spotting useful connections and timing projects well.
  • An unusual sports tournament: coaches prepare their own teams across several events, deciding where to spend effort and where to conserve it. The challenge could be reading opponents and matching strengths to the schedule.
  • Invention fair: inventors assemble simple machines or AI-inspired systems to solve shared challenges. The challenge could be discovering clever combinations of visual rules, with no specialist knowledge required.

Choose based on the world and activity that inspire the designer, then explore whether the working mechanisms suit it. The seeds do not establish scoring, a production budget, or how a nine-player game resolves.

City theme and family accessibility

Design direction to explore: make the city’s opportunities familiar and tangible, such as opening a bakery, improving transport, or supplying a neighborhood. Historical context can give projects meaning without requiring players to know historical facts.

Use a small set of clear actions, readable symbols, and simple calculations. These are assistant recommendations, not established rules. The designer points out that Splendor is recommended for ages 10+ and sees similar rules complexity as a promising way to reach families too. Explore an adult-appealing experience accessible to older children; this is a working audience hypothesis, not a tested age rating or a conclusion about market size.

Audience validation: check whether intended younger players understand secret commitments, make their own meaningful choices, and remain engaged after spending effort without earning a reward. Compare rules explanation, decision time, and enjoyment in adult and mixed-age groups. A comparable rule count alone does not establish comparable difficulty.

Roadmap — for reference

  1. Discover the desired player experience through guided questions, using reference games where useful.
  2. Explore theme seeds from the designer’s interests before settling objectives and rewards.
  3. Brainstorm concrete concepts that connect theme, objectives, and mechanics. Revisit the working mechanics as needed; borrowing mechanics is optional.
  4. Prototype and test pacing at two, four, and nine players.
  5. Refine rules, then develop visual designs.

This is a tentative sequence, not a checklist to tackle all at once.
