Design brief — agreed preferences

Reference games

Splendor and Azul are favorites; City of the Great Machine is also enjoyable. The appeal is logical decision-making and the way one player’s actions change what others need to do. These are references for enjoyable experiences, not a commitment to copy their rules or player roles.

Other enjoyable games and activities include chess, solving a Rubik’s Cube, and Texas Hold’em poker. What specifically appeals about each is still to be explored; their inclusion does not establish a preference for bluffing, betting, hidden information, or any particular puzzle structure.

Requirements

  • An original game shaped by the designer: favor their own ideas and creative direction. Borrowing existing mechanics is optional; combining the named reference games is not the design brief.
  • Individual competition: every player plays for themselves.
  • Mixed information: combine public information with some private information or choices. What stays hidden and when it is revealed remain undecided.
  • Limited randomness in both forms: include a little randomness in the opportunities available before decisions and a little uncertainty in action outcomes, allowing calculated risks. Exact frequency and stakes remain open; luck should not dominate.
  • Satisfying decisions: include opportunities to find non-obvious solutions, anticipate other players, and combine things in unexpected, effective ways. All three appeal to the designer; their relative emphasis is undecided, and every move need not provide all three.
  • Competition through self-benefit: an action that improves your own position may disadvantage others as a consequence. Avoid actions whose main purpose is intentionally damaging an opponent. This refines the earlier blanket restriction on harm; specific effects remain undecided. Avoid making someone feel singled out as a victim.
  • Simple rules, strategic depth: aim for roughly Splendor’s level of rules complexity, with a quick explanation that friends can understand easily.
  • Several viable approaches: repeated play should reveal interesting possibilities rather than one dominant winning strategy.
  • Responsive strategy: the best choice should depend on what opponents do. Rock–paper–scissors illustrates this relationship; it is not a request to use that mechanic.
  • Replayability and accessibility: experienced players should stay interested, while newer players retain a reasonable chance of winning.

What the game should feel like

  • Logical and deliberate: decisions and their consequences should be understandable.
  • Connected: other players’ actions should meaningfully influence your choices.
  • Satisfying to handle: value physical component quality, especially the feel of Splendor’s chips. Specific components and production budget are undecided.
  • Comfortably social: leave some mental space for friends to catch up during play; constant intense concentration should not be necessary.
  • Forgiving: a mistake should not automatically remove a player’s realistic chance of winning. How much recovery is possible remains to be tested.
  • Engaging from the opening: offer interesting choices early, without a repetitive preparation phase in which everyone feels obliged to do the same thing.

Avoid

  • Luck dominating the outcome. A little randomness in both opportunities and action outcomes is welcome.
  • Trading, bargaining, or negotiation as required gameplay. Casual conversation is welcome.
  • Continuous high-pressure concentration, such as the shape-fitting experience described in Ubongo.
  • A single dominant strategy or an early mistake deciding the whole game.
  • A repetitive opening built around everyone accumulating discounts before the interesting decisions begin.
  • Actions mainly designed to damage another player, especially repeated targeting that leaves one person upset or creates arguments. Disadvantaging others as a consequence of improving your own position is acceptable.

These are design targets, not validated properties of a game we have built.

Interaction — agreed direction

Core principle: what other players do should matter to your decisions. You can make a plan, but you need to reconsider your tactics as the situation changes.

  • Splendor example: a shared market contains a card you have been preparing to buy for several rounds. Another player takes it before your turn, changing your options.
  • Azul example: other players take tiles you wanted, changing what is available and what makes sense for you to choose.
  • The appeal: respond to opponents and changing opportunities rather than follow the same plan regardless of their actions.
  • Mechanism still open: a shared market or tile draft could produce this experience, but neither is required or chosen.

Design question for later testing: how can a disrupted plan lead to an interesting alternative while preserving our goal that mistakes and setbacks remain recoverable? At nine players, we will also need to test how much can change between meaningful decisions.

The reference examples describe the desired experience; they are not complete rules summaries.

Boundary clarified: the designer accepts harm that arises as a consequence of acting for your own benefit, but dislikes intentionally damaging others as the purpose of an action. The concern is repeated victimization, sadness, and arguments. Design around incentives for self-benefit, and test whether players nevertheless find repeated targeting rewarding. Exactly which effects are acceptable remains open; earned progress is not subject to the blanket protection previously inferred.

Progression — preference clarified

The designer does not particularly enjoy Splendor’s permanent-discount buildup. In their experience, the opening feels boring because everyone builds up in a similar way. They also perceive this as the clear winning approach in the base game without the expansion they refer to as “Silk Road.” This records their experience and assessment, not an independently established claim about Splendor’s strategy balance.

Design direction: make the opening interesting and avoid a repetitive buildup that feels compulsory. Splendor-style discount accumulation is not a feature we need to reproduce.

Still open: other forms of progression, upgrades, and combinations. This preference does not rule them all out.

Agreed goals

  • Support approximately 2–9 players.
  • Keep the game enjoyable at nine players, with little frustrating waiting.
  • Target about 40 minutes of play for 2–4 players, excluding the rules explanation.
  • Target no more than about 25% longer playtime at nine players: about 50 minutes maximum against the 40-minute baseline. These are targets to validate in playtests.

Ideas to explore — not decisions

Simultaneous choices, fixed rounds, shared events, and interaction with a limited number of opponents are possible ways to reduce waiting. None has been chosen. The earlier negotiation idea has been set aside because it conflicts with the stated preferences.

Preferred directions after comparison: simultaneous private action selection with shared reveal, and shared opportunities with individual responses. Explore these first. They could be combined, but the designer has not selected a final combined structure. Resolution must also scale well; simultaneous selection alone does not guarantee the session-length target.

Theme, core mechanics, victory conditions, and the exact rules complexity are still open. Session-length targets are recorded above.

Design challenge to test later: give beginners a reasonable chance and allow recovery from mistakes while keeping luck low and skilled decisions meaningful. Also keep interaction readable at nine players without requiring everyone to track eight opponents in detail.

Round structures to explore — not final rules

These are assistant-proposed structures for comparison. They can be adapted or combined as the designer develops the game’s own central activity.

StructureWhat players doWhat to test
Secret choice, shared revealEveryone privately commits to an action, then reveals together. Outcomes depend partly on the combination of choices.Anticipating opponents could be satisfying. Collisions need fair, quick resolution and useful alternatives; nine separate resolutions could still be slow.
Shared opportunity, individual responsesReveal one common situation or set of options. Everyone simultaneously decides how to use it for their own position.A common prompt can keep rounds short, but players’ responses must affect shared opportunities or rewards to preserve meaningful interaction.
Choose and passEach player chooses one option from a small personal set, then passes the remaining options to a neighbor. Repeat together.Choices influence what others receive. Keep the number of selections bounded as player count increases; check whether interaction feels too limited to neighbors.
One player leads, everyone respondsA rotating lead player chooses an action category. Everyone gets a related action or response, with a possible advantage for the leader.Everyone participates in each turn. Nine lead turns per cycle could still extend play too much; total action count and fair access to leading need testing.

All simultaneous structures can still wait for the slowest decision. Test both selection time and resolution time, while leaving room for conversation.

Possible combined round — illustration only

  1. Reveal a small set of shared opportunities.
  2. Everyone privately chooses how to respond at the same time.
  3. Reveal the choices together and resolve their interaction.

For example, several players might choose the same opportunity. How that affects rewards is still open. Compare a reduced benefit for everyone with a known risk of no benefit, and invite other ideas. Neither is a decided mechanic. The number and capacity of opportunities must also work at nine players.

Roadmap — for reference

  1. Discover the desired player experience through guided questions, using reference games where useful.
  2. Brainstorm a few concrete concepts shaped by the designer’s ideas; borrowing mechanics is optional.
  3. Explore theme and mechanics together.
  4. Prototype and test pacing at two, four, and nine players.
  5. Refine rules, then develop visual designs.

This is a tentative sequence, not a checklist to tackle all at once.
