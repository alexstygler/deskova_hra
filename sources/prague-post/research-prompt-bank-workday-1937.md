# Research prompt: bank workdays and postal timing, 24 February–3 March 1937

Copy the text below into a chat with web research. This prompt requests evidence; it does not establish the events or procedures it asks about.

---

Act as a historian of Czechoslovak banking and postal operations and a board-game research assistant. Research Prague from **24 February to 3 March 1937 inclusive**, especially bank correspondence, daily workloads, dispatch timing and the tariff change effective 1 March. Do not assume an extraordinary banking emergency occurred: a well-supported ordinary working week may be the right conclusion.

Read these project materials first:
- https://alexstygler.github.io/deskova_hra/
- https://alexstygler.github.io/deskova_hra/zdrojove-materialy.html — corrected primary DOCX and all three map pages.
- https://alexstygler.github.io/deskova_hra/sources/prague-post/Prazska_potrubni_posta_1936-1937_vyzkum_pro_hru.md — supplied research report; independently check important new claims rather than citing another AI as evidence.

Identify inaccessible material. Separate a contemporary source from a modern transcription, plausible reconstruction and invented game rule. Cite exact pages, dates and links beside substantive claims.

## 1. Ordinary bank work and the timing of correspondence

Investigate commercial-bank work in Prague in 1936/37: receiving mail, logging and distributing it, preparing replies, authorising or signing outgoing letters, customer-counter hours, bookkeeping, payment/cheque processing, clearing or settlement and dispatch arrangements. Which activities and institutional roles are actually documented?

- Were outgoing letters prepared throughout the day or concentrated at particular times?
- Did incoming correspondence normally receive priority before new outgoing business? Was there a universal rule or only institution-specific practice?
- Could ordinary work cause some banks to dispatch in the morning and others in late afternoon? Seek evidence rather than inventing a universal schedule.
- Distinguish a bank's opening hours for customers, staff working hours, private tube-station availability, public postal hours and actual dispatch times.
- Identify relevant bank manuals, office instructions, dispatch/receipt registers and archival finding aids. A finding aid identifies a research lead; it does not prove the content of unread records.
- Distinguish preparing a response, approving it, posting it, and delivering it. No assumption that every recipient replies immediately.

## 2. Private tube connections versus public collections

Explain separately:
1. Public street-mailbox collections.
2. Posting at a public counter.
3. An institution using its private connection to Jindřišská.
4. Dispatch from the central office into the wider tube network.
5. Ordinary final delivery rounds or special messengers.
6. Return posting and delivery of replies.

The supplied materials report hourly collection of special public mailboxes in the post-1932 regime, long tube-service hours at selected offices, and earlier final ordinary delivery rounds. Verify their dates, scope and exceptions. Do not confuse a last mailbox collection with a last walking-delivery departure. Resolve, or retain as unresolved, the differing 12:00/14:00 same-day posting limits.

For private connections, determine whether a bank could dispatch when ready, had to use a returning capsule, request clearance, follow scheduled departures, or await line availability. Which rules are documented for the chosen period? Were dispatches batched? What happened when a recipient institution was closed? Do not infer a private dispatch timetable from public mailbox intervals.

## 3. What actually happened during these dates?

Build a dated table for all eight calendar days, with correct weekdays. Establish Sunday/Saturday practices, banking and postal holidays, and differences between customer and transport services where evidence permits.

Search contemporary newspapers, official gazettes, bank publications and authoritative archives for events creating plausible correspondence pressure: payment or settlement deadlines, month-end work, interest/coupon dates, tenders, import/export or foreign-exchange procedures, public-finance announcements, and postal instructions. These are search hypotheses, not facts to assume.

For every candidate, distinguish announcement date, effective date and actual business deadline. Explain the bank or customer affected, the message it could motivate and the strength of evidence. Do not invent a crisis or an urgent duty shared by all banks. Do not import events from later in 1937 into this February/March scenario without labelling them as alternatives for another scenario.

## 4. Tariffs and financial consequences

Verify the 1 March 1937 change. The supplied report gives messenger surcharge 1→2 Kč in the inner delivery zone and 5→2 Kč in the outer zone, with public pneumatic surcharge unchanged at 40 h/item. Private participant charging of 40 h/capsule is a separate matter. Check original or authoritative tariffs, exact units, territorial scope and whether contract terms remain unknown.

When was the change announced? Which date determined the applicable charge for a posted item, a reply posted later, or a requested service? Do not assume every unfinished business case attracted a retrospective surcharge. Separate that fictional game experiment from historical postal billing. Explain examples only where supported, avoiding automatic addition of private capsule fees to public item fees.

## 5. Translate findings into a few manageable game alternatives

Our confirmed direction:
- 2–9 banks, individual competition, one shared map; historically exact nine-bank roster remains unresolved.
- Several short working days. Most tasks span more than one day.
- Everyone plans the whole day simultaneously in secret, including action order; everyone reveals the complete plan at once, followed by public resolution.
- Personal task offers arrive each morning and have expiry dates. Accepted tasks are visible.
- Credits represent handling effort, paid once in full when starting a task, including its future reply processing. Credits reset each morning; unused credits expire. Amounts remain open.
- Money pays postal costs in both directions. Game capsules have no letter-count limit. Batching can save dispatch costs; a full historical charging model is unresolved.
- Recipient response times are known to players; network congestion may still affect actual return time.
- Urgent and flexible tasks coexist. Late urgent income decreases by days or bands, not yet numerically defined.
- Abandonment is permitted without refunds. One identical end-game fee applies to every unfinished accepted case, including abandoned cases; amount remains open.
- Any tariff-change date and charges are visible from the start. A special unfinished-case surcharge is merely parked for testing, not a historical fact.
- Target 40 minutes at 2–4 players and at most about 50 at nine, with room for conversation. No deliberate attacks or compulsory negotiation.

Compare three or four timing models, including shared dispatch windows, bank-specific mail-ready times, and automatic queues with deadlines. Explain which aspects have historical support and which are abstractions. None is currently selected.

Can ordinary bank work affect mail-ready time without adding another accounting game? Can early/late availability produce several defensible plans rather than random punishment? If preparation of replies affects workload, explain how to represent it without charging the already-prepaid handling credits twice. Distinguish bank reply work from customer response time.

Give one small worked day for two contrasting plans. Count decisions, manual movements and public resolution steps, and explain likely nine-player bottlenecks. Do not claim a duration is achieved without playtesting. Avoid simulating every historical hour or requiring players to replan during the day.

Be candid if evidence cannot establish a routine, event, timetable or tariff. Historical plausibility is not proof of an actual exchange. Keep the resulting rules teachable to people with no banking knowledge.
